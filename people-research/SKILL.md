# People Research

## Purpose
Turn a list of names into an audience playbook. The method: map who decides what, research each person's public footprint in parallel, infer what would make them vote/engage/remember you (marked as inference), and write per-person talk tracks — openers grounded in their actual work, what to show in a 1-on-1, what not to say, and the follow-up move.

Scope boundary: this skill produces the playbook. It does not pick what to build (that's `hackathon-seam-hunting`) and it does not write the demo itself (that's `demo-ready`). It can be called as a step from either.

## Workflow

### 0. Gather inputs (input contract)
- The people: names + roles/affiliations as listed by the event or deal.
- The context: what you're bringing (project, ask, story) and what you want from each interaction (win a prize, land an intro, get real feedback, be remembered).
- Time available for the research pass (it parallelizes well — spend it on deciders first).

### 1. Map the power
Before researching anyone, write down who decides what: prize deciders vs general panel vs audience; hiring manager vs broker vs referrer. Research time goes to deciders first. If no decision structure is published, say so — don't invent one.

### 2. Research pass (per person, parallelizable)
Public sources only: web search, X/Twitter, blogs, conference talks, GitHub, papers. Never attempt a login (LinkedIn included — use search snippets). For each person find:
- Role + one-line background.
- Public writing/talks/posts, especially recent (last ~year) and on-topic.
- Personal builder footprint: GitHub repos, side projects, papers, maintained OSS.
- Technical interests or strong opinions stated publicly.
- "Not found" is a complete and acceptable answer. Never invent a profile, repo, post, or talk. Ambiguous identities (common names) stay unattributed — report the ambiguity, don't guess.

### 3. Infer triggers — marked as inference
For each person, one line: what would make them vote for / engage with / remember you. Every such line is labeled inference and must follow from their role + public material, not from wishful thinking. Separate what they said (evidence) from what you conclude (inference).

### 4. Write the talk tracks
Per person, grounded in their actual work — never flattery:
- **Opener:** a question or observation about something they actually did/said/built.
- **1-on-1 demo beat:** what to show in the first 60 seconds for this person specifically.
- **Don'ts:** what loses them (hand-waving, vapor, unmeasured claims — varies by person).
- **Follow-up:** the one question to ask them or the move to make after.
Add a short section on feedback tactics: the two questions that extract real signal from anyone — "what's the weakest claim in this?" and "what would make this a yes for you?"

### 5. Prioritize
Order the people by leverage for your goal: deciders first, then the best feedback-givers, then the rest. Networking time is finite; the playbook says who gets it. For hackathon/event execution — the build-ready tripwire, the screen-as-pretext move, and the calendar-fill — see `references/event-conversion-playbook.md`. After a win — formalities/evidence capture and parallel conversion — see `references/post-win-playbook.md`.

## Output Contract
- Decision/power map (who decides what, or "not published").
- Per person: role line, public footprint, builder footprint, trigger inference (labeled), talk track (opener, demo beat, don'ts, follow-up).
- Priority order with one-line reason each.
- Explicit "not found / ambiguous" entries where research came up empty.

## Operating Rules
1. Public sources only; no login attempts, ever.
2. Never invent. "Not found" beats a guess; ambiguity is reported, not resolved by guessing.
3. Every inference is labeled inference and traceable to evidence.
4. Talk tracks are grounded in their work, not flattery. A generic compliment is worse than no opener.
5. Deciders get the research time; don't gold-plate the audience.
6. Keep it dense — one line per field per person. This is a playbook, not a biography.
